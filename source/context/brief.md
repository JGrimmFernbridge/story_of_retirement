## Core frameworks (Jonathan's own — treat as the course's interpretive spine)
- **Natural Order Economics** — three principles: *Not Poverty* (sufficiency as a floor), *Community* (risk shared/pooled), *Generativity* (contribution sustained across the lifespan). Classical world embedded them; industrialization broke all three at once.
- **The Conflation** — two questions merged into one: the humanitarian question ("how do we care for people as they age?") and the structural question ("what systems do we build to do it?" / "how does each individual fund 30 years of not working?"). Today's answer: "You."
- **Anomaly vs. Model** — 1950–1980 worked because of unrepeatable conditions (five economic pillars: manufacturing hegemony, demographic dividend, cheap energy, capital markets with limited global competition, productivity growth; five social pillars: institutional trust, intergenerational compact, deferred gratification culture, geographic stability, nuclear-family design spec). It was never studied as a model; it was replaced by a counterfeit that kept the name "retirement."
- **The Great Risk Transfer** — DB pension → 401(k): risk moved from institutions to individuals and was sold as freedom.
- **Bismarck's pension as vacancy mechanism / exit subsidy** — age 70 in 1889 (lowered to 65 in 1916); political pacification of socialists.
- **Accountability without proximity** — decisions made by people who never live with their long-term consequences; not bad actors, bad consequences.
- **"Who gave you that dream?"** — the client question; retirement as an incepted idea.
- **The Kick** (Inception) — the book as the jolt that wakes the dreamer. Recurring line: *"Are you watching closely?"* (The Prestige).
- **The Two-Person Lens** — at each threshold year, a 60-year-old exiting work and a 20-year-old entering it: same moment, two realities.
- **The Future Poor thesis** — ~9 of 10 people under 50 earning under $150K are on track to live at or below poverty level in old age ("sub-50, sub-150").
- **The Nine Seeds** (exact names and numbers — do not change):
  1. Segregation & Boundaries — who deserves protection; exclusions (agricultural & domestic workers 1935). Older than retirement itself.
  2. Government Involvement — begins with Bismarck 1889; retirement as political management; ambivalence between protection and abandonment.
  3. 65 = Old and Done — the age became cultural identity endpoint, never updated as longevity grew.
  4. Market Growth — first appears in the Roaring Twenties (placed at 1924): belief that equity markets reliably rise and can be a savings mechanism.
  5. Search for Leisure — first appears in the 1920s consumer economy; blooms post-war (Sun City 1960 / "golden years"); retirement as earned vacation.
  6. Get Rich Quick — speculation, opportunity, possibility, speed (1920s margin buying; returns in 80s–90s, day trading, crypto).
  7. Two Americas — rich vs. rural / industrial-urban-unionized vs. agricultural-rural-domestic; system built for the first, excludes the second.
  8. The Promise — corporate pension era (post-1946): "work here 30 years and we'll take care of you." Failure feels like betrayal (a moral category).
  9. Individualism — 1981, first 401(k) plans + Reagan/Thatcher: collective security → individual responsibility. Converts systemic failure into personal failure. "The plan didn't fail you. You failed the plan."
  Grouping (from Jonathan's prior analysis): foundation seeds (Segregation, Two Americas), culture seeds (65=Old, Leisure, Promise), mechanism seeds (Government Involvement, Market Growth, Get Rich Quick, Individualism). Codify (1980–2010) is where "all nine seeds are in full bloom."

## Existing Two-Person Lens portraits (Jonathan's working doc — reuse/adapt where they fit)
- 1890: born 1830 vs 1870 — 60yo never thought about retiring; 20yo is the generation that will eventually be told they must.
- 1935: born 1875 vs 1915 — SS designed for the 60yo's desperation, perfected for the 20yo's comfort.
- 1950 (book, Crucible chapter): born 1890 vs 1930 — "The 60-year-old who signed it knew exactly what they were buying. The 20-year-old who inherited it had no idea what it cost."
- 1960: born 1900 vs 1940 — the anomaly, though told they're the template.
- 1985: born 1925 vs 1965 — 60yo cashing out a promise; 20yo handed a calculator and told good luck.
- 2010 (book, Collapse chapter "The Two Clocks"): born 1950 vs 1990 — 60yo still has time to panic; 20yo has already moved past grief into improvisation.
- 2025: born 1965 vs 2005 — 60yo trying to exit a system that no longer functions; 20yo never entered it.

## Voice & standards for course content
- Course-guide register: clear, vivid, rigorous; for undergraduates; consistent with Jonathan's thesis but never sloppy with facts. Where the book makes an argument, attribute it ("the book argues…", "Grimm's framing…").
- Accuracy is non-negotiable: verify every date, statute name, statistic and figure with WebSearch/WebFetch against authoritative sources (ssa.gov/history, congress.gov, archives.gov, govinfo.gov, dol.gov, bls.gov, fraser.stlouisfed.org, census.gov, federalreservehistory.org, CBO, SSA Trustees Reports, academic sources). If unsure of a number, omit it rather than guess. Use the most recent official projections (e.g., latest Social Security Trustees Report — check whether a 2026 report exists).
- Intellectual honesty for grading: every module's discussion questions must include at least one that asks students to test, complicate or challenge the book's argument with evidence (e.g., Social Security dramatically reduced elder poverty; defenders of the 401(k) model).
- Political topics: describe actors and policies factually; keep the book's critique attributed to the book.
- Do not copy long passages from web sources; paraphrase. Primary-source URLs should be stable official pages.
