Most books don't begin with the ending - especially history ones. Most movies don't either. Yet, the great ones begin where you least expect only to untangle everything as the story unfolds.

Are you intrigued?

Are you watching closely?

You cracked open a book with the title The Concise History of Retirement and are probably expecting a book that reads chronologically. After all, that is how we normally read history. Past to present. Then to now. What happened at a certain date and time.

That makes perfect sense! I took a lot of history classes too! We know what to expect when we see a history text book. Events, people and places in a sequential order. 1492 to 1776 to 1941.

There is so much that makes up "history." There are major figures and regular people. There are key events and the ordinary. There are often two sides, winners and losers and "History is written by the victors" — often attributed to Winston Churchill and something we can probably intuit makes sense and leaves the history we have been told wanting.

We grow up learning history as a series of facts. We learn about different empires over time. We learn about ancient civilizations. We learn about the technological advancements that sprang up. Cave paintings, the pyramids, the printing press, the steam engine, the light bulb. The tests we have to pass in school asks, "what happened on what date." We fill in the bubble indicating the correct answer on our scantron paper... Oh no! I just aged myself and may have outed you. I am so sorry despite fond memories of a personal history that includes a beloved scantron and maybe an overhead projector.

Yet, history is so much more than facts. It is consequential in that all that has happened before has influenced where we are today and where we are headed. And that brings us back to Churchill when he said in 1948, "Those that fail to learn from history are doomed to repeat it."

## Why a Concise History of Retirement?

I am glad you asked and to answer that I will give you three concise answers. One is personal. One is practical. The final one is problematic.

First the personal. No one asked me to write this book but I know you were demanding this type of work be done! This is the conclusion after all and you end this book by being so glad you picked it up. "Life changing!" you exclaim. For me, this began as part of my first book The Future Poor: How families and communities can survive the looming retirement crisis. I had a short chapter in there that was the seed of what you now hold. I felt I was at the start of seeing something important with the history of retirement. I had the scaffolding but that was about all. It was enough to make the points I needed for that book but I knew there was much more to do. The more I worked with clients and discussed the trajectory of retirement the more the history became illuminated. Echoes of the past were showing up everywhere from hundred year old rules, to tax laws of previous generations, to ideological shifts and financial products created half a century ago. Each of these threads was woven together into this thing we now call retirement. I wanted to understand it deeply and here at the end you do too!

I mentioned the second reason for a concise history of retirement being my practical side. I like short books rather than massive treatments of things. History is a massive thing to unpack which is why each of the characters in our story about retirement have multiple biographies and each of the events mentioned here fill volumes of encyclopedias. I wanted to shrink that down and hit the high points and illustrate the arc of history that ends here with you and your moment in the story. It is important for you to see where you are now and step forward into what is next.

This book is also concise because retirement as a thing, as an idea, as something people do is relatively new. The history of retirement is concise and so the book should be too! Most are surprised to realize we are only about 130 years in on this idea. That may seem like a long time for you and me but in the scope of human history, that is a blip. As something new in the course of human history, it needs to be understood.

And now the problematic reason for this book and a call back to my first book. It's in the title and you've already seen the end. Retirement, which I broadly define as exiting work at 65 and living your remaining years off your money, is a catastrophe. My original thesis of The Future Poor is that 9 out of 10 people under age 50 (sub-50) and making less than $150,000/year (sub-150) are going to be living at or below poverty level in their senior years. That has not changed. That is not good. That is a catastrophe. The rest of this book is why.

Are you watching closely?

When we understand the history and see the different seeds of ideas that gave us this catastrophic ending we can join with those that already see, undo the tangled knot and build something better. You get it and why you are passing this book along to everyone you know.

## Your Advice for New Readers

Here at the end you have a better sense of how this book is to be read. Like we do when we tell a friend about a TV show, a movie or our favorite restaurant - we want to set them up for an enjoyable experience. Perhaps we offer some tips or insider information about things we have come to learn from experience. But we also know the truth of what C.S. Lewis said about expectations being everything. We can’t oversell the movie and create disappointment. We’ve all been told something is the best only to encounter it and walk away feeling “meh” about it. That is why we do the balancing act of informing and not giving away the plot.

"The parking is terrible so park around the corner. And get there early before it gets packed."

"Do you mind the sight of blood on screen? You do? Ok. Then you probably shouldn't watch The Pitt."

"You know it starts off slow but if you make it to episode 3 or past the first 10 minutes it really picks up pace."

"Make sure to ask what they are serving that is not on the menu."

These are the things we naturally do and so here is what I imagine you are telling others about how to read this particular book and what to pay attention to. You love this book and cannot wait to share it with friends, family and co-workers but you want them to walk away with the same experience. I cannot blame you for that! So here is what you tell people as you pass this along.

I ran into M. Night Shyamalan story. If you tell your friend Bruce Willis is dead the whole time in the Sixth Sense the movie doesn’t have the magic it is supposed to carry. In a way I have told you this fact but you aren’t familiar enough with the details for it to set in. Retirement is Dead and has been the whole time might be revealed but you haven’t seen the detail of it being cold and seeing your breath when Willis is around. But watching it over again you will see all the details you missed along the way.

## "Seeds. Yes seeeeds. (emphasis on the D) Not C's"

What you'll come to find out is retirement as we know it is a collection of ideas that began as seeds that grew together and grew into bigger ideas. It is not one single seed that gave rise to what we now think of as retirement — "exit work at age 65 and live off your money." There is a lot of range in that simplistic definition but that is what most of us think that term means. Eventually, several seeds all grew into developed ideas that form the foundation for our current concept of retirement. Seeing them over time is really interesting.

## "Christopher"

Yup — another "C."

One of the great things about this book are movie references as they are able to illustrate realities we encounter in our daily life in ways words fail. Narrative has always been the language to capture the deeper things of life. It used to be books and evolved into cinematic story telling. They also connect to popular culture and stories we all can resonate with. This is often more powerful than anything I could do with charts, graphs or logic. Story and narrative connect much deeper than basic prose cannot.

So who is the greatest of these story tellers?

George Lucas, of course. He gave us Star Wars — the original trilogy. The best mythological story and movies ever made.

But that isn't Christopher.

You are correct (another c) — I am talking about Christopher Nolan and the intrigue with his work since Memento. Memento was the first of Nolan's movies that didn't follow a traditional chronological approach. This movie runs backwards in order to understand the ending. Or the beginning. Or the ending. I don't know but I know it works.

Then there is his movie Inception. Dreams within dreams that made people question reality in ways not felt since The Matrix. More about this later.

But that was only the start. Does anyone understand Tenet? A movie that I think is running forward and backward at the same time?! Who knows?!

This book pays homage to Nolan in my own way and began with the question, "Are you watching closely?"

Some of us know that from Nolan's movie The Prestige which is actually an adaptation of a book by the same name written by another "C" — Christopher Priest. If you haven't seen it yet you need to check it out. But that question becomes a guiding question as you journey through this history and continues to sit in your mind as you see how the story unfolds.
